# 01 — Concepts, modèles et contre-exemples (élève : anglais)

**Goal / Success question.** *Can I make my draft clearer for a reader before I polish it? Can I show and explain the changes?*

## The four-pass revision routine

1. **MEANING [M] — Is the message accurate and complete?** Name the intended reader and purpose; check that the main point is present, facts from the draft are kept, and events or steps make sense. If a fact is unclear, mark a **question for the author**; do not invent an answer.
2. **COHESION [C] — Can the reader follow the ideas?** Put ideas in a helpful order, remove needless repetition, choose truthful connectors, and make pronouns refer clearly to people or things.
3. **CONVENTIONS [F] — Can the reader read the sentences easily?** Check capitals, end punctuation, spelling, verb forms, and sentence boundaries. Conventions help readers, but perfect spelling cannot repair a confusing message.
4. **RE-READ — Does the new draft still say what I meant?** Read as the intended reader. Show **before → after → because** for each major change.

**Revision** means rethinking what a text communicates and how it is organised; it is not merely proofreading. **Cohesion** means explicit, accurate links within and between sentences (logical sequence, repeated key words, pronoun references, connectors). **Conventions** are shared spelling, punctuation, capitalization and grammatical practices suited to the audience. A writer's valid dialect or voice is not itself an error; favour changes that resolve a real obstacle to understanding.

**Annotation key (works without colours):** `[M]` meaning, accuracy, intended audience or main point; `[C]` order, pronoun or connector; `[F]` grammar/spelling/punctuation. Write **what** changed and **why**; e.g. `[C] "It" → "The invitation": the reader now knows what arrived.`

## Worked example 1 — School garden, short report

**Original fictional draft:**
> Our school garden has three raised beds. we planted bean seeds on Tuesday. The garden has three raised beds. They need water each morning.

**Reader/purpose:** a classmate who wants a clear update. The author confirms that **“They” means the seeds**, not the beds; without confirmation, ask first.

**Passes and revisions:**

| Pass | Before → After | Writer's reason |
|---|---|---|
| [M] | Two identical sentences about the beds → mention the beds once | The same fact is enough once, leaving room for what happened. |
| [C] | `They need water each morning.` → `The seeds need water each morning.` | `They` could mean beds or seeds. The author confirmed seeds. |
| [C] | `we planted bean seeds on Tuesday.` → `On Tuesday, our class planted bean seeds there.` | The time connects clearly to the action and `there` points back to the garden. |
| [F] | `we planted` → `On Tuesday, our class planted` | Begin the sentence with a capital letter. |

**Revised draft:**
> Our school garden has three raised beds. On Tuesday, our class planted bean seeds there. The seeds need water each morning.

**Check:** The revised version retains three beds, Tuesday planting and daily watering. It invents no new event. Other clear, fact-preserving revisions are possible.

## Worked example 2 — Narrative sequence

**Original fictional draft:**
> At last, Jo found the key. First, Jo looked under the desk. Jo open the cupboard. the key was inside.

**Reader/purpose:** a friend reading how the search ended. The author confirms that Jo found the key **inside the cupboard**.

| Pass | Before → After | Why |
|---|---|---|
| [M] | Opening with `At last` → opening with `First, Jo looked under the desk.` | Readers should meet the beginning of the search first. |
| [C] | `Jo open the cupboard.` → `Then Jo opened the cupboard.` | `Then` accurately continues the sequence. |
| [M/C] | `At last, Jo found the key.` + `the key was inside.` → `At last, Jo found the key inside.` | Combines two related facts without repetition. |
| [F] | `open` → `opened`; `the` → `The` if sentence retained | Correct past-tense form and sentence capitalization. |

**Revised draft:**
> First, Jo looked under the desk. Then Jo opened the cupboard. At last, Jo found the key inside.

**Check:** Order and details are preserved; no unreported locations are added.

## Two counterexamples — not successful revision

1. **Proofread-only error.** `At last, Jo found the key. First, Jo looked under the desk. Jo opened the cupboard. The key was inside.` This fixes forms but still begins with the ending: **meaning/order remains confusing**. Repair by checking chronological order before spelling and grammar.
2. **Inventing a cause.** Original: `The path was wet. We played indoors.` Faulty revision: `We played indoors because a storm flooded the playground.` The draft does not mention a storm or flooding. A safe alternative: `The path was wet. We played indoors.` If the author confirms the reason, it may then be added; otherwise **do not invent it**.

## Reader-friendly checklist

- [ ] I know **who** will read the piece and **why**.
- [ ] The main point, facts and sequence make sense; missing facts are questioned, not invented.
- [ ] Ideas connect with clear nouns/pronouns and truthful linking words.
- [ ] I checked spelling, capitals, punctuation and grammar **last**.
- [ ] I can show `[M]`, `[C]`, `[F]` changes and explain why each helps.
