# 01 — Notions et exemples | qc-pri-ale-007

**Objectif immuable (`obj-1`) :** Formuler une appréciation littéraire fondée sur des éléments de l’œuvre.  
**Preuve (`crit-1`) :** Défendre une appréciation et reconnaître une autre lecture possible.  
**Public :** élèves du primaire ayant accès à de courts récits anglais; accompagnement de lecture modulable.  
**Usage :** corpus et corrigés originaux, fixes; ne pas montrer les réponses des preuves finales pendant l’évaluation.

## Three useful reading tools

1. **Theme**: an idea about people or life that a work explores, beyond what happens. A theme can be phrased as a claim (*Sharing a story can strengthen a connection*), not merely a topic (*sharing*). Several themes can coexist; a reader supports a possible theme with details.
2. **Narrative choice**: a decision about **how** a story or poem is told: a first-person *I*, a third-person narrator, what is shown or left unsaid, the order of events, imagery, repetition, an open ending. Explain **what effect that choice might have** on readers; do not invent the author's private intention.
3. **Textual evidence**: a precise word, phrase, action or line in the supplied work that helps justify an interpretation. Cite a line number and explain the link. A quotation alone is not an argument. A paraphrase with the correct line is also acceptable.
4. **Appreciation**: a considered response to the work's effect or craft, not just "I like it." Include a judgement, a theme or idea, a narrative choice, evidence and a plausible alternative reading.

**Difference:** *Plot* = what happened. *Theme* = what the work explores. *Opinion* = what the reader thinks. *Evidence* = what in the work can support or challenge that opinion. A theme is not the lesson every reader must accept.

## A four-part response frame (English)

> I appreciate / question how the text presents **[theme]**. The choice to **[narrative technique]** makes **[effect]** noticeable. In line **[n]**, **[short quote or accurate paraphrase]**, which suggests **[reasoned connection]**. Another reader might see **[plausible alternative]** because **[different detail]**; I still think **[reason]**.

The frame may be removed once students can argue on their own. **Grammar** should support intelligibility; accent, dialect and the pupil's personal taste are not rated as evidence of literary quality.

## Worked example A — a short narrative

**“The Spare Key” — texte original, lignes numérotées**

1. Maya found a brass key beneath the library return slot.
2. She held it up in the quiet hallway, but nobody came.
3. A note tied to it read, "For whoever needs a quiet place."
4. Maya could have slipped the key into her pocket.
5. Instead, she left it on the librarian’s desk with a note: "Someone may be looking for this."
6. As she left, the reading-room light was still on.

**Teacher think-aloud:**
- *Not just the plot*: finding a key and returning it is an event; **acting responsibly with something that may belong to someone else** is a defensible theme.
- *Narrative choice*: the narrator reports Maya's actions without explaining who the key belongs to. The last image of a lit reading room leaves room for wondering.
- *Evidence*: line 4 says Maya **could** have kept the key; line 5 shows what she **chooses** instead. These contrastive actions support the appreciation.
- *Sample appraisal*: *I appreciate the quiet ending because it leaves space for the reader to think about responsibility. The contrast between what Maya could do (line 4) and what she does (line 5) suggests that caring for strangers matters. Another reader could see a story about belonging, because the message on the key mentions "a quiet place" (line 3). That reading is possible, but Maya's choice is the strongest evidence for my response.*

**Check:** The quoted words "a quiet place" are actually in line 3. The opinion is about a text, not the student's private beliefs.

## Worked example B — first-person voice

**“The Last Kite” — texte original, lignes numérotées**

1. I thought a kite needed strong wind to be worth flying.
2. When the sky turned still, I folded mine away.
3. My little brother asked, "Can we run instead?"
4. We crossed the field, holding the string together.
5. The kite rose for a moment, then tipped into the grass.
6. I laughed so hard that I forgot to be disappointed.

**Teacher think-aloud:** The *I* narrator begins with an expectation (line 1) and ends with an unexpected feeling (line 6). The first-person choice lets us hear the change directly. The theme could concern enjoying a shared experience more than achieving a perfect result.

**Sample appraisal:** *I like the first-person ending because the narrator admits a change in feelings. The kite fails to stay up in line 5, yet the narrator laughs in line 6. This contrast shows that the afternoon matters more than a successful flight. Another reader might focus on teamwork, since the children hold the string together in line 4. Both ideas fit; my emphasis is the narrator's change.*

## Counter-examples and repairs

- **Counter-example 1:** *"The theme is a key, and the story is good because I like keys."* → *key* names an object, not a theme; the judgement lacks evidence. **Repair:** *The story explores responsibility; the contrast between lines 4 and 5 makes Maya's choice meaningful.*
- **Counter-example 2:** *"The author definitely wants us to think that Maya owns the reading room."* → The excerpt never says this. **Repair:** *The last light (line 6) could suggest that a place is waiting for someone, but the owner of the key is unknown.*
- **Counter-example 3:** *"The kite never flies."* → Contradicted by "rose for a moment" (line 5). **Repair:** *It rises briefly before falling; this makes the narrator's laughter more striking.*
- **Counter-example 4:** *"The first-person narrator proves every reader must enjoy the ending."* → Narrative voice does not force a uniform emotional response. **Repair:** *The "I" voice makes disappointment and laughter explicit, but readers may respond differently.*

## Evidence procedure

1. Read the whole short text for literal meaning. 2. Underline an exact action or phrase. 3. Label the narrative choice. 4. Write a defensible theme and a judgement of its presentation. 5. Tie the evidence to the claim with *because/this suggests*. 6. Test a second plausible reading **against a different detail**. 7. If the text contradicts the claim, revise it.

**Vocabulary mini-glossary:** *theme* = idée explorée; *narrative choice* = procédé/choix de narration; *textual evidence* = appui textuel; *open ending* = fin ouverte; *first-person narrator* = narrateur à la première personne; *imagery* = images créées par les mots; *interpretation* = lecture fondée sur le texte.
