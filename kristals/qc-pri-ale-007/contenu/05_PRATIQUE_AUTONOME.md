# 05 — Pratique autonome | qc-pri-ale-007

**Consigne commune (English):** *Read the whole text. Write or record your own appreciation: one supported theme claim, one narrative/poetic choice and its effect, precise evidence with a line number, and a different plausible interpretation. Explain why your reading remains reasonable.* Perform each task independently **without the guided answer frames**. A scribe may transcribe word for word; do not suggest interpretations. Suggestions below are **corrector exemplars** to keep hidden until feedback.

## A1 — Family stories (narrative)

**“The Tin Box” — texte original, lignes numérotées**

1. Sam kept every ticket from the small town fair in a tin box.
2. One day, a younger cousin asked to see them.
3. Sam opened the lid and told the story of each ride.
4. By evening, the tickets had not changed, but the stories belonged to two people.

**Task:** *Do you think the ending is effective? Support your appreciation and make room for another reading.*

**Corrector exemplar:** *I appreciate the contrast between unchanged tickets and shared stories (line 4); it suggests that sharing memories connects people. The ending makes an ordinary object feel important. Another reading could stress remembering the past, because Sam saved every ticket in line 1. I prefer the connection reading because two people share the stories at the end.*

**Criteria:** not merely retelling; accurate line 1/4 evidence, discussion of contrast or another defensible choice, reasonable alternative. **Unacceptable assertion:** the cousin buys the tickets (not in text).

## A2 — Possibility (poem)

**“Cloud Ladder” — texte original, lignes numérotées**

1. A ladder of clouds leans over the hill.
2. I cannot climb it; I watch it grow still.
3. The wind takes the steps one by one.
4. I walk home with the thought of the sun.

**Task:** *Appreciate the cloud image. Explain what the changing image may mean and offer another reading.*

**Corrector exemplar:** *I enjoy the image of a ladder made of clouds because it presents an impossible climb as something worth imagining. Line 1 describes the ladder, and line 3 lets the wind remove its steps. This could explore imagination that changes quickly. Someone else might see learning to accept limits, since the speaker cannot climb it in line 2.*

**Criteria:** interpretation anchored to image, accurate wording, not claiming a real ladder exists.

## A3 — First-person perspective (map narrative)

**“The Bench Map” — texte original, lignes numérotées**

1. I drew a map so visitors could find our garden.
2. At first I marked only the shortest path.
3. Then I noticed the bench beneath the old maple.
4. I added a winding path past the bench and the small pond.
5. My map took longer to follow, but it showed more places to stop.

**Task:** *Evaluate the narrator's final choice. How does the first-person voice help communicate an idea? Name another possible interpretation.*

**Corrector exemplar:** *I value the narrator's change from the shortest path (line 2) to a longer one with interesting stops (lines 4–5). Because the story uses "I," we hear a change in the map-maker's priorities. It may be about noticing the journey, not just reaching a goal. Another reading might emphasize making a map useful for different visitors; line 1 supports that possibility.*

**Criteria:** distinguishes first-person voice from theme; evidence must not imply the map is faulty.

## A4 — Comparing choices (two endings)

**“Two Endings” — texte original, lignes numérotées**

1. Shared opening: Jo planted a seed in a cracked cup. Every morning, Jo checked the soil.
2. Ending A: One green leaf appeared. Jo carried the cup to the sunny window.
3. Ending B: Nothing appeared. Jo filled a fresh cup with soil and tried again.

**Task:** *Which ending do you appreciate more, A or B? Explain the effect of the different endings with evidence and give one reason another reader might prefer the other ending.*

**Corrector exemplar:** *I prefer ending B, because Jo decides to try again when nothing grows (line 3). It gives the story a theme of persistence. The writer's choice to show a new attempt rather than quick success changes my response. Another reader may prefer A because the first leaf (line 2) makes patience feel rewarding.*

**Criteria:** both endings compared accurately; preference never scored; reasoned evidence and alternate reading scored.

## Common correction and feedback rules

- Use **the rubric in `evaluation/GRILLE_MAITRISE.md`**, not right/wrong interpretation marking. Several interpretations can earn full credit when supported.
- If the pupil cites a wrong line, ask them to find the exact action or phrase and reconnect it to the claim.
- If they summarize only, prompt: *What is meaningful about the way the writer shows it?*
- If they insist that alternative readers are wrong, request a line that supports a second possible reading, then evaluate both against text.
- Record selected evidence and independence; these four practices are **not** the two formal proof contexts (`e1` and `e2`).
