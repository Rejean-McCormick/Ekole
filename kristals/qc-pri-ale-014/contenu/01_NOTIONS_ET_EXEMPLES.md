# 01 — Notions, exemples et contre-exemples | `qc-pri-ale-014`

**Observable target (`obj-1`):** Participate in an oral exchange and then present a reasoned idea in English.  
**Independent evidence (`crit-1`):** Sustain an exchange and explain an idea with an example.

## 1. Precise concepts, explained in accessible English

- **Turn-taking** means listening while a partner speaks, responding to the actual message, letting the partner speak again, and, when appropriate, asking a related question. Speaking twice without engaging the partner is not a genuine exchange. Useful sentences: “What do you think?” / “Could you explain that?” / “I understand. I would choose…”
- **Presentation** means giving listeners a short, coherent account of a choice or proposal. For this Kristal the structure is **idea → reason → example**; a simple closing phrase may follow. This is oral communication to an audience, not a memorized paragraph read aloud while ignoring a question.
- **Reason** means an explanation that makes a claim relevant to a goal or condition: “I suggest a quiet reading space **because** it would help students concentrate.” “Because it is the best” is not informative without further support.
- **Example** is a specific plausible illustration of the reason: “For example, a group could take turns using it during independent reading.” Examples may be invented but must fit the **fictional** prompt.
- **Intelligibility** is whether a listener can understand the main message. Pronunciation varieties and accents do not have to resemble a single native-speaker norm.

**Important difference:** one can be an excellent presenter yet not listen to a partner, or be a friendly conversationalist without ever giving a reason and a concrete example. Both parts must be observed separately.

## 2. Worked example A — Fictional library choice

**Scenario:** A class has space for either a small display of new books or a set of comfortable reading seats. The group wants to encourage reading.

**Oral exchange (4 alternating turns):**

1. **Partner:** “What would make our library more welcoming?”
2. **Learner:** “I would add two reading seats. What would you choose?”
3. **Partner:** “I would display new books. Why are the seats helpful?”
4. **Learner:** “They give students a comfortable place to read. Your display could help students discover books too.”

**Short oral presentation:** “I recommend adding two reading seats because a comfortable space can encourage reading. For example, two students could sit there during quiet reading time.”

**Annotation:** Turn 2 answers the question and gives the partner a genuine invitation; turn 4 answers *why* and acknowledges the different proposal. The presentation states an idea, a relevant reason, and a plausible example. There is no unique aesthetic model or requirement to prefer seats over a display.

## 3. Worked example B — Fictional school fair

**Scenario:** A school fair has room for one calm activity. Pupils are choosing between a puzzle table and an art table.

1. **Partner:** “Which quiet activity should we offer?”
2. **Learner:** “I suggest a puzzle table. Would you enjoy that?”
3. **Partner:** “Maybe. How would people work together?”
4. **Learner:** “They could solve puzzles in small groups and take turns placing pieces.”

**Short oral presentation:** “A puzzle table is a good choice because students could solve a problem together. For example, a group of four could complete one puzzle while sharing the pieces.”

**Why it works:** A listener can follow the topic and causal link, and the example is specific. A different well-explained activity could be just as correct. Clear everyday English is preferred over complicated unsupported vocabulary.

## 4. Counterexample A — One speaker dominates

**Attempt:** Partner asks, “What activity would you choose?” Learner says, “A puzzle table. It is fun. I really like puzzles. Puzzles are the best. I choose puzzles,” without pausing or responding to the partner's next words.

**Problem:** A single uninterrupted speech does not demonstrate turn-taking. “Fun” may be a preference, but it does not yet explain how the activity meets the fictional group's needs. **Repair:** Stop after an answer, ask “What do you think?”, let the partner answer, then respond to the new information.

## 5. Counterexample B — Circular reason and missing example

**Attempt:** “We should have a reading corner because reading corners are good. For example, it would be good.”

**Problem:** The reason only repeats the conclusion; the “example” contains no specific situation. **Repair:** “We should have a reading corner because students need a quieter space to focus. For example, two readers could use it during free reading time.”

## 6. Practical distinctions

| Learner phrase | What can be observed | What cannot be assumed |
|---|---|---|
| “Could you repeat the question?” | Functional request for clarification | Poor mastery merely because clarification was requested |
| “I agree because we can share the materials.” | Response + relevant reason | Evidence of an example without a specific situation |
| “For example, four students could share one table.” | Concrete example | Evidence that the learner listened to a preceding turn unless an exchange was observed |
| An accent different from the evaluator's | Normal variation in spoken English | Lack of intelligibility without evidence from actual listener comprehension |

**Safety and inclusion:** All scenarios concern fictional, low-stakes choices; no personal disclosure or pressured public debate. Voice-recording is optional and needs appropriate consent; a teacher's observation notes suffice.
