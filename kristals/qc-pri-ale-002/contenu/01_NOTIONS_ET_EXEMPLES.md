# 01 — Core notions, worked examples and non-examples

**Target / Cible** — “Apply English letter–sound correspondences to decode new regular words and show the clue used.” (`obj-1`, `crit-1`). Read English words **from print**. Explanations for the teacher may be in French; directions for children are in natural English.

## Definitions (with testable boundaries)

1. **Phonics:** linking written spelling patterns (letters or groups of letters) to spoken phonemes to read unfamiliar print. It is **not** guessing a word from a picture or knowing the story.
2. **Grapheme:** a letter or several letters that together spell one sound in a particular word. In *cash*, `c | a | sh` are three graphemes for /k/ /æ/ /ʃ/; `sh` is a single grapheme with two letters. A grapheme is a spelling unit, not a letter name.
3. **Sound blending:** combining separate speech sounds in order into a spoken word: /s/ + /ɪ/ + /t/ → *sit*. This *process* is different from a **consonant blend**: `sl` in *slip* still spells two sounds (/s/ and /l/); `sh` in *shut* spells one /ʃ/.
4. **Short vowels in this controlled corpus:** `a` /æ/ (*cat*), `e` /ɛ/ (*bed*), `i` /ɪ/ (*sit*), `u` /ʌ/ (*sun*), in the selected word pronunciations. Do **not** assert that a vowel letter always has only one sound in English or that dialectal vowel realizations are all identical.
5. **Left to right:** look at the graphemes in sequence → retrieve their sounds → join sounds smoothly → say the full word → show the grapheme used as evidence. Avoid appending an extra /ə/ to /t/ or /p/ in isolation.

**Scope:** common regular closed-syllable English words, with optional taught `sh` /ʃ/ and `ch` /tʃ/. No promise that English spelling is fully phonetic; deliberately exclude *said, one, was, have, give*, and untaught multi-syllable patterns from scored tasks.

## Worked example 1 — Three single-letter graphemes

**Card:** `m | a | p`. **Teacher think-aloud:** “The letter `m` spells /m/, `a` spells the short /æ/ here, and `p` spells /p/. I slide the sounds together: /m/–/æ/–/p/ → **map**. My clue is the short `a` in the middle, supported by the consonant sounds.” **Expected student demonstration:** read *map* with recognizable sounds and point to `a` or tell its phoneme. **Not** acceptable: say only “em–ay–pee” and never read the word.

## Worked example 2 — One grapheme with two letters

**Card:** `f | i | sh`. **Model:** “`f` = /f/, `i` = /ɪ/, `sh` = /ʃ/. The two letters `s` and `h` work together. /f/–/ɪ/–/ʃ/ → **fish**. I used `sh` for its single /ʃ/ sound.” **Correctness:** 4 letters represent 3 phonemes in this word, not 4; acceptable if read with another established English accent while the target correspondence is intact.

## Counter-example 1 — Letter naming is not decoding

“*Cat* is **see–ay–tee**.” This names `c,a,t` but has not joined /k/ /æ/ /t/ into *cat*. **Repair:** point `c | a | t`, say pure /k/ /æ/ /t/ and blend to *cat*. The printed clue is `c` spelling /k/ here.

## Counter-example 2 — Breaking the digraph

“`sh` in *shut* is /s/ + /h/; therefore it has four sounds.” **False for this word.** `sh | u | t` maps to /ʃ/ /ʌ/ /t/, three phonemes. **Repair:** put both letters in one tile, produce /ʃ/, then blend all three sounds. **Important distinction:** `sl` in *slip* spells two consonant sounds /s/ + /l/; a consonant cluster is not automatically a digraph.

## Two quick comparisons

- **Decoded evidence:** “I read *bed* because `e` represents /ɛ/ and the other letters give /b/ and /d/.”
- **Unsupported guess:** “I think *bed* because there is a bed in the picture.” An illustration may support meaning later, but does **not** establish decoding.

**English pupil routine:** “Point to the spelling. Say the sounds. Blend them. Read the word. Tell or show your clue.”
