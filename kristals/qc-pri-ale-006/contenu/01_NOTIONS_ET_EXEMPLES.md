# 01 — What can we infer? Notions, worked examples and counter-examples

**Learning goal / `obj-1`:** *Infer an unstated intention or piece of information from an English passage.*  
**Success evidence / `crit-1`:** *Formulate two inferences, each supported by text clues.*

## Three essential terms

| Word | Precise meaning | Observable reader action |
|---|---|---|
| **Text clue** | Words or details that the text actually gives: an action, sequence, contrast, description, number, or choice of words. | Point to or quote the exact clue; do not add an invented detail. |
| **Inference** | A reasonable conclusion **not explicitly stated** in the text, built from one or more clues and limited background knowledge. | State what *may* be true and test it against the full passage. |
| **Justification** | The explanation connecting a particular clue to the conclusion. | Use “I infer ... because the text says ..., which suggests ...”. |

**A fact versus an inference:** “The door was locked” is a direct fact when printed. “Someone may have arrived after closing time” is an inference *only* if other details support it; being locked alone does not prove the arrival time.

**Four-step strategy — C.L.U.E.:** **C**heck what is stated; **L**ocate one or two clues; **U**se a reasonable connection (“why would these details matter?”); **E**xplain while respecting uncertainty. A second inference needs a *different* idea; a repeated sentence is not a second inference.

## Worked example 1 — Narrative: “Before the Bell” (original fiction)

> At 8:58, the hallway was almost empty. Noor held a folded permission slip in her hand. She looked at the classroom door, then back at the office sign. She walked to the office instead of going into class. At the counter she asked, “Can someone sign this before nine?”

**Inference A:** Noor probably needs a signature soon. **Text clues:** “held a folded permission slip,” “asked, ‘Can someone sign this before nine?’” **Connection:** the request is about signing a slip and specifies a near deadline. The text never explicitly says *she feels nervous*, so that should not be asserted.

**Inference B:** Noor chooses the office because she expects someone there can help. **Text clues:** “looked at the classroom door, then back at the office sign”; “walked to the office instead of going into class.” **Connection:** her deliberate change of direction suggests she thinks the office is the better place for the task. It does not prove which adult will sign.

**Teacher think-aloud:** *I do not guess the whole story. I underline what the author actually wrote. I say “probably” when the detail does not make my idea certain. I link my idea to those words.*

## Worked example 2 — Informational: “Garden Notice” (invented local notice)

> **Community garden update:** A wheelbarrow delivery is planned for Saturday at 10 a.m. Please keep the west path free of bags and bicycles between 9:30 and 11 a.m. The east path will remain open for visitors. Garden activities will start as usual at noon.

**Inference A:** Organizers likely want the west path clear so the wheelbarrow can pass safely. **Clues:** “wheelbarrow delivery” and “keep the west path free of bags and bicycles.” **Why it follows:** the vehicle needs space; the text does not explicitly state that reason.

**Inference B:** Visitors can likely still access the garden during the delivery period. **Clues:** “east path will remain open for visitors” and “west path” restriction. **Why:** an alternative route remains available. Avoid saying *all visitors will definitely arrive on time*, which the notice cannot establish.

## Counter-example 1 — Copying the text is not inferring

Text: “The east path will remain open.”  
**Bad response:** “I infer the east path will remain open.”  
**What fails:** the statement is copied directly.  
**Repair:** “Visitors may still be able to enter during the delivery, because the east path stays open even while the west path must be clear.”

## Counter-example 2 — Imagining an unsupported motive

Text: “Talia placed the book on the table and closed her bag.”  
**Bad response:** “Talia hates reading and wants to go home.”  
**What fails:** neither emotion nor destination has textual support.  
**Repair:** “The text does not show why Talia closed her bag. I need another clue before suggesting a motive.”

## A justifiable answer is not a secret answer key

Two responses can earn credit if each is compatible with the passage, cites suitable clues and explains the connection. Reject readings that contradict explicit facts or replace textual evidence with stereotypes or unrelated personal anecdotes. Check whether the inference is truly *unstated*. Good readers sometimes say: “The evidence is insufficient.”

**English oral/written sentence frames:** “A clue is ...”; “I infer that ... because ...”; “This detail suggests ...”; “Another possibility is ...”; “We cannot tell whether ...”.
