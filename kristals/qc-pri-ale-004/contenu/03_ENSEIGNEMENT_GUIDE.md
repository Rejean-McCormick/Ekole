# 03 — Teacher-led reading and think-aloud

## Preparation (2–3 minutes)

State: **“Today we will find a story's character, key events, plot and evidence.”** Display four prompts: **WHO? → PROBLEM OR CHANGE? → ACTION? → RESULT?** Make a two-column chart: **My claim | Words in the text that support it**. Explain that story details are not automatically important, and that a summary must not invent anything.

## Model 1 — Read for the overall story
**The Lantern Path** (original instructional story, M0)

> After school, Nia noticed that the path to the garden was dark. She wanted younger students to reach the garden safely. She asked the caretaker for permission to hang battery-powered lanterns along the fence. At first, the lanterns were too far apart, and the last turn was still dark. Nia moved two lanterns closer to the corner. The next evening, the whole path was easy to see. Nia smiled when the younger students found the gate without stopping.
**Read 1, without pausing:** “I notice Nia is central because she notices the problem and acts. The path is dark. She tries spacing lanterns, but the last turn stays dark. She changes the arrangement; the younger students find the gate.” Model 3 arrows: **dark path → change lantern positions → safe-to-see route**. Do not infer that the path is physically hazard-free; the text says it is easy to see.

**Read 2, evidence hunt:** Underline “the last turn was still dark”; underline “Nia moved two lanterns closer to the corner.” Say: “My claim is that Nia fixed the placement problem. I can point to the second line. That is text evidence.” Distinguish an exact citation from a plausible guess.

**Teacher model of a short English summary:** “Nia wants younger children to find a dark garden path. When her first lantern arrangement leaves one corner dark, she moves two lanterns, and the children can see the way to the gate.” **Linked evidence:** “Nia moved two lanterns closer to the corner.”

**Why this response works:** It identifies Nia, includes cause and response in order, states the outcome and quotes a detail. It does not claim that Nia rebuilt the gate or used solar energy.

## Model 2 — Transfer the routine to a different event
**The Paper Bridge** (original instructional story, G1)

> At the school fair, Juno built a paper bridge for a toy car. The bridge bent when the car reached the middle. Juno folded the sides of a new strip of paper and placed two blocks beneath its ends. This time the bridge held the car. Juno showed her classmates how the folds made the bridge stronger.
**Think-aloud:** “Juno is the main character. Her first bridge bends. She folds a fresh strip and props up the ends. Now the bridge holds the toy car.” A concise plot statement is **design fails → design improves → test succeeds**. “The bridge held the car” supports the result; it does not prove how much weight a real bridge could carry.

**Compare summaries:**
- Too broad: “Juno learned things.” **Repair:** name *which problem* and *which action*.
- Too detailed: “There was a fair, a strip of paper, two blocks, a toy car and classmates.” **Repair:** connect important changes into a causal sentence.
- Accurate: “After her paper bridge bends, Juno folds a new piece and supports it so her toy car can cross.” **Evidence:** “This time the bridge held the car.”

## Guided oral check (stop before independent work)

1. Ask “Is the fence a character in Nia's story?” **Key:** no, it is part of the setting.
2. Ask “What changed between Juno's first and second tests?” **Key:** she folded a new strip and placed blocks under the ends.
3. Ask “Which line supports your claim?” **Key:** a directly relevant quotation or identified text action, not a personal guess.
4. Ask learner to improve: “Nia built a gate.” **Key:** replace unsupported claim with lantern movement and its visible result.

**Release to guided practice** when the learner can locate at least one correctly linked source detail and describe the problem/response order. If not, return to one paragraph and fill the organizer jointly. Reading at speed alone is not the mastery criterion.
